# Judge Q&A preparation

Short answers, each backed by a command in README "Verification" (questions 16 to 22 are about design choices and rules, so they point to the docs instead). Say the number, then the source. If a judge finds a real limit, agree and name the fix; never stretch a claim.

| # | Question | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | How do you know the Sun and Earth positions are right? | We compare against NASA JPL Horizons at 5 sites (3 polar, Apollo 11, Chang'e 4) for 30 days: worst error 0.0047°, about a hundredth of the Sun's width (`npm run validate`). Injecting a 0.2° error makes the check fail. |
| 2 | How do you know the sunlight numbers are right? | NASA's LOLA team publishes Sun and Earth visibility maps for the pole (PGDA product 69, Mazarico et al. 2011). With their definitions we agree to a median 0.3 percentage points for the Sun and 0.2 for Earth over 131 points and 18.6 years (`npm run crosscheck`). Rotate our map 180° and it fails. |
| 3 | Then why is your Shackleton rim 90% and NASA's map 61% at the same spot? | The 61% is the average of 16 NASA pixels around our point. The crest is one or two 120 m pixels wide, so most of that block is crater wall in shadow. NASA's best pixel within 240 m is 85.8%. Ours is about 4 points higher, consistent with 480 m terrain slightly lowering the skyline. That is the point of our neighbourhood map: 0% to 87% within 2.4 km. |
| 4 | Is 480 m terrain enough? | For the horizon, yes: what blocks the Sun is ridges kilometres to hundreds of kilometres away, and our 120 m cross-check agrees to 0.3 points in the median. For landing hazards, no: boulders and metre slopes need 5 m data (NASA PGDA product 78). We say that in the app; slope here is over 960 m and NASA's limit is over 100 m, so real slopes are steeper. |
| 5 | What about relay satellites? | The challenge defines communication as direct-to-Earth, and NASA's Artemis III requirements say "assume no comm relay is available". The far side shows why relays exist: Chang'e 4 sees Earth 0% of the year. Adding a relay orbit is our first extension. |
| 6 | Why is Haworth rim (Malapert massif side) your best balance? | Over one year it has the Sun and Earth up together 69.0% of the time, the highest of our presets; its longest Earth blackout is 4.8 days against 14.4 for Shackleton rim (`npm run results`). The name says "Malapert massif side" because the best-lit point of Haworth's rim ring lies there. |
| 7 | What does the score mean? | 0.4 × sunlit % + 0.4 × Earth % + 20 × (1 − longest night / stay). Power and comms weigh the same; the night term rewards short battery gaps. It is a ranking aid, not physics, so the raw numbers sit next to it. |
| 8 | What are the Artemis III checks? | Five of NASA's six "Key Artemis III Landing Requirements" from the Artemis overview NASA gave Space Apps: within 6° of the pole, landing slope under 10°, EVA slope within 2 km under 20°, direct-to-Earth link, lighting. The sixth, landing within 100 m of a target, is navigation. Shackleton rim scores 4 of 5: the crater wall within 2 km is 33.7°. |
| 9 | How is this different from Quickmap, Moon Trek and JPL Horizons? | The challenge statement names those as built for specialists. We answer one planning question on one screen: which site, which date, with countdowns, a best-date jump, Artemis checks and a shareable link, offline, in three languages. |
| 10 | Does it handle libration and the lunar seasons? | Yes: Sun and Earth are rotated into the Moon's real orientation every hour (IAU model), which includes libration; that wobble is exactly why Earth rises and sets at the pole. The year view shows the seasons in the date planner. |
| 11 | Does it work outside the south pole? | Yes: the whole Moon at 7.6 km, plus 474 m around 17 landing sites (Apollo, Luna, Chang'e, Chandrayaan-3, SLIM, IM-1, Blue Ghost). Apollo 11 sees Earth 100% of the year but has a 14.8-day night. |
| 12 | How were the landing site coordinates chosen? | Missions: published coordinates (Wikipedia's landing list citing LROC and NSSDCA; IM-2 from its own article because the list page has it wrong). Pole presets: crater centres from the USGS Gazetteer, then the best-lit point on each rim ring, computed, not typed in. |
| 13 | What would you do with another month? | Relay satellite visibility; 5 m terrain around Artemis III regions; solar array orientation for real power in watts; thermal; a class mode on top of Mission mode. |
| 14 | How did you use AI (SI)? | Claude in Claude Code wrote most of the code and checks; every number was then checked by a command against NASA references, and each check was broken on purpose to prove it can fail. Details in docs/SI-usage.md, as the rules require. |
| 15 | Was this built before the hackathon? | Answer factually: the repository dates show what was done when, and docs/SI-usage.md lists it. Do not argue; show the dates. |
| 16 | Why only one mission with fixed numbers? | A first-time visitor must understand it without reading anything else. When we tested an earlier version, we judged that it had too many variables and dashboards. Now there is one decision per step: the landing day, the stay, the 2 day battery and the 3 day radio limit are fixed, so the only thing you change is the landing spot and you can see why the robot survived or was lost. Anyone who wants every variable (any place, any date) uses the full planner, one button away. |
| 17 | Why is there no 3D? | Three reasons. The terrain is 480 m per pixel, so a 3D view would look more exact than the data is. A landing descent needs an altitude and a speed that we do not have, so they would be invented numbers. And in a ten minute descent the Sun and Earth barely move, while the challenge is about how they change over days. The statement allows "2D maps, 3D surface visualizations, or other creative representations", and we chose 2D maps, a real 360° skyline, live shadows and a guided mission. We considered a 3D landing simulator and decided against it. |
| 18 | Other teams have 3D globes. What is different about yours? | Do not name or judge other teams; say what ours does. It can be played in two minutes by someone who has never seen it, with one decision per step. It runs by opening one file, with no internet and no install, so a school with a bad connection can use it. It is in English, Russian and Kyrgyz. Its story is the challenge's own, "launch delays ... force mission planners to reconsider their original landing date and location": the launch slips 21 days, so replan. And every verdict comes from the engine checked against NASA JPL Horizons and NASA's illumination maps. A 3D globe and our tool answer different questions; we are not claiming ours is better. (Context: we read several public repositories for this challenge on 13 Nov 2026; several have 3D globes and one has a small game.) |
| 19 | Are the 2 day and 3 day limits NASA rules? | No. The 2 day battery, the 3 day radio limit and the 21 day launch delay are teaching values chosen by our team. Say so first, before anyone asks. The NASA rules in the tool are the five Artemis III checks in the full planner (NTRS 20250009323), and the slope there is measured over 960 m, not NASA's 100 m, so real slope is steeper. |
| 20 | Why 22 July 2027? | The team chose it from the data. We searched the year of computed results for a landing day when both the place and the date change the outcome. On 22 July 2027 the Ridge and the Second rim survive (never dark, Earth hidden at most 2.5 days), the Plateau is lost to a 4.4 day night, and the Crater wall is dark and too steep (32°). After the 21 day delay, on 12 August 2027, the Ridge and the Second rim are lost because Earth is hidden for 4.2 and 3.6 days, and the Plateau survives. The browser test (`npm run smoke`) fails if this stops being true. It is a teaching scenario, not a real mission. |
| 21 | Is this a game or a tool? | Both, on one engine. Mission mode is the game: anyone can play it in two minutes, you choose where a robot lands, and real NASA data gives the verdict. The challenge statement names "an educational game" as a valid format "as long as it makes rapid site evaluation clear and efficient", so the full planner is the tool behind it, one button away and unchanged. The game's numbers are the planner's numbers, and the planner is the part we validated against NASA. We do not simulate a flight: the idea is to teach by deciding. |
| 22 | Does it tell me how much water ice there is, or which vehicle to use? | No. The tool has no water or ice data and no vehicle specifications, so it does not guess. It tells you whether a site and date give the Sun, Earth contact and slope that a mission needs. |

## Numbers to know by heart

- 0.0047°: worst error against JPL Horizons
- 0.3 points: median difference against NASA's illumination map
- 86.9% sunlit, 14.4 days without Earth: Shackleton rim
- 69.0% Sun and Earth together: Haworth rim (Malapert massif side)
- 0% to 87% within 2.4 km: why a few hundred metres matter
- 0% Earth: Chang'e 4 (far side)
- 2 days of battery, 3 days without Earth, Sun within 24 hours of landing, 21 days of delay: our teaching values in Mission mode, not NASA's
- Eleven checks in the mission report: 4 must pass (night, radio, first day Sun, slope under 10°), 2 NASA Artemis III site rules (20° within 2 km, within 6° of the pole), 5 for information
- Can it predict a future day? Yes: the any-day step computes Sun and Earth positions for any date from 1950 to 2100 from the orbit model; the terrain does not change. We validated against JPL Horizons over 30 days, not over the whole range, so say that
- On 22 July 2027 the Ridge and the Second rim survive (never dark, Earth hidden at most 2.5 days), the Plateau is lost to a 4.4 day night, and the Crater wall is dark and too steep (32°). After the 21 day delay, on 12 August 2027, the Ridge and the Second rim are lost because Earth is hidden for 4.2 and 3.6 days, and the Plateau survives.
- The 3D view (by teammate steppekid) uses LOLA 80 m terrain and JPL DE440; our engine uses LOLA 480 m and astronomy-engine. Both give the same eight verdicts for the mission (`npm test`)
- 69° and about 15 hours of Sun over Naryn on 22 July 2027, against at most 1° at the four spots
- 30 seconds or 7 slides: the demo limit in the Project Submission Guide
