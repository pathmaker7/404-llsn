## Project Name

Lunar Landing Site Navigator

## High-Level Project Summary

We built a free web app that answers one question for the Moon's south pole: if a solar robot lands here on this day, does it survive 14 days? It is made for students in Kyrgyzstan and Central Asia: in English, Russian and Kyrgyz, with a ready 45 minute lesson, replays of real landings such as Chandrayaan-3 and IM-1, and four games on the real model, where a student builds a lander, keeps its battery alive, hunts for ice and takes a quiz. For planners it compares 15 sites near the south pole, or any point you add, on any day from 2021 to 2031, using NASA LOLA terrain and JPL ephemerides: a 3D Moon, the view from the ground with the Sun and Earth where they really are, and a calendar of which days work. Every landing gets one of three answers: GO, NOT SURE with a rate from 25 to 100%, or DON'T GO. The rate is the share of four re-checks (a day earlier, a day later, terrain 0.2° higher and lower) that give the other answer. Sites are ranked by a score, 8 spots 1 km around the target are judged, and a line says which Deep Space Network dish sees the Moon. Our Sun and Earth positions agree with NASA JPL Horizons to 0.0047 degrees. It matters because a launch that slips by one day can turn a safe landing into a lost one, and students here can now see why in their own language.

## Detailed Project Description

**What it does.**
1. The first screen shows the whole Moon in 3D, gliding to the south pole. Under it, one row per site and one block per landing day: a green block means a solar robot landing that day survives 14 days, a red block means it is lost. Every row ends with its rank and a score, for example `11 / 30  #1  37`: the score is the green landing days divided by all landing days in the period shown, times 100, rounded, and sites with equal scores share a rank. The answer panel says it in words, for example "Rank 1 of 15, score 37 of 100 (Nov 15 to Dec 15, 2026)". Rank and score follow the period buttons and the battery and radio limits. Play moves the sunlight over the Moon.
2. Fifteen sites: six from NASA's Lunar Surface Data Book and nine the team picked on NASA LOLA terrain. Click anywhere on the Moon to drop your own lander there: it gets its own row of landing days at once. Coordinates can also be typed, and known landings picked (Apollo 11 and 17, Chandrayaan-3, IM-1, Chang'e 6, IM-2).
3. Any day from 2021 to 2031, from a two week view up to the whole period. Zoomed out, the cycles show: one short window a month while Earth is above the horizon, months with almost no good day while the Sun is in its low season (Feb to Jul 2027 at the best site), and the whole pattern returning about every 354 days, 12 lunar months, measured in the app's own verdicts for 9 of the 10 sites that have any good day.
4. Click a day and you stand 2 m above the real terrain at that site. The Sun and Earth sit at their true positions; the Sun's height, "sunset in" and "Earthset in" are shown.
5. One answer panel: MISSION SURVIVED or MISSION LOST, one sentence why, the next 120 landing days, and eleven checks with a tick or a cross (longest night, longest radio silence, first sunlight, slope, and NASA Artemis III site rules for slope within 2 km and distance from the pole).
6. Launch slipped? Type how many days late the launch is, and one line says which sites still survive a landing that day, or the next good day and how many days later.
7. Your own mission: change how many days the battery lasts without Sun and how many days of radio silence are allowed; every site and day is judged again. The table downloads as CSV, and the page address keeps your sites and limits so a comparison can be shared as a link.
8. Replay a real landing: the app stands you on the site at the moment of touchdown and shows what really happened next to the model.
9. **Solid or borderline.** Under the checks of every verdict the panel says "Solid: the answer holds one day earlier or later, and with the terrain 0.2° higher or lower." or, for example, "Borderline: this landing is lost one day later." The landing is judged four more times: a day earlier, a day later, the terrain horizon 0.2° higher and 0.2° lower, each recomputed hour by hour from the site's own horizon. In the 400 landing days scanned by the test of this check, 11 change their verdict on terrain alone. The 0.2° is the team's own test value, not a NASA figure. Every landing also gets one of three answers at the top of the panel: GO (it survives and all four re-checks agree), DON'T GO (it is lost and all four agree), or NOT SURE with a rate, the share of the four re-checks that give the other answer (25, 50, 75 or 100%), coloured from yellow to deep orange.
10. **What if we land 1 km off.** A small compass in the answer panel: the target in the middle, 8 spots 1 km around it, solid green where a landing survives and hollow red where it is lost, and one sentence, for example "2 of 8 spots 1 km around also survive." or, when the target is lost, "1 km to the north east this landing survives." The 8 spots follow the same survival rule and the same limits as the target. They are judged on NASA LOLA terrain averaged to 320 m, coarser than the 80 m of the prepared sites, so this tests a 1 km miss, not 100 m; the compass says so in its tooltip. It works only within about 235 km of the south pole.
11. **Dish on Earth.** One line in the answer panel, for example "Dish on Earth: a dish can see the Moon for over 99% of the hours Earth is in view. At landing: Goldstone." For every hour Earth is in view from the site, the Moon must be 10° or more above the horizon at one of NASA's three Deep Space Network sites (Goldstone, Madrid, Canberra). It is geometry only: the 10° is the team's planning value, not a NASA rule, and the line never changes the verdict.
12. **Motion that is ours.** Light arrives from the side and shadow slides over, as at the Moon's south pole, where the Sun stays low. When the list is first ready, one line of light passes over it from the left and the rows come in behind it. A verdict that changes gets one sweep: a warm line of light when it says survived, a soft shadow when it says lost. Rows glide to their new rank when the period changes. Nothing bounces, spins or loops, nothing in motion blocks a click, and all of it is off when the visitor asks for reduced motion.
13. English, Russian and Kyrgyz, and a ready 45 minute school lesson in the same three languages: https://lunar-navigator-explained.web.app/lesson.html

**Four games for students.** Each one is a different way to touch the same answer. Every green, red, "survived" or "lost" in them comes from the same survival rule and the same NASA based Sun and Earth data as the main grid; none has a rule of its own. A first visit explains itself: a tour of five steps lights the landing calendar, the lander card and the three game buttons in turn, the first press on Ice hunter, Quiz or Keep me alive shows a "How to play" card (the goal in one sentence, then what to press, in numbered steps), and a "How to play" button starts the tour again.
14. **My lander.** A student builds a lander: a lander on legs, a rover or a hopper, in one of six colours, with a name, and wearing a Kyrgyz ak kalpak, a Kazakh kalpak, an Uzbek doppi, a Tajik toqi or no hat. Click a landing day in the table, or click the Moon, and the lander drops from where you clicked onto the Moon: a thruster flame slows it, dust puffs up as it lands, and then it lights up green and waves its hat if that landing survives, or frosts over and goes dark if it is lost. A speech bubble gives one sentence in the student's language saying why (for example, the Sun was gone longer than the battery lasts). In the 3D view of a site, the lander stands on the ground near the viewer under the real Sun, with its name and "alive" or "frozen" for the chosen landing day. The lander and the hats are drawn in code, and the choice is kept in the student's own browser.
15. **Keep me alive.** The button is on the first screen (it opens the best site of the period shown, on its next surviving day) and on every site. The student picks a landing day, or presses "Try a green day" or "Try a red day", and watches the 14 days play in about 40 seconds (speeds 1x, 2x and 4x). The battery fills while the Sun is up and drains in shadow; radio bars show while Earth is above the horizon; the day counter and a strip of Sun and Earth hours fill in. The lander in the game is the one the student built in My lander. It ends with "14 days survived!" or the reason the lander was lost, and says that the answer is the same as the map and the verdict panel.
16. **Ice hunter.** On the map, the student presses "Ice hunter" and taps a spot near the south pole. The app checks the Sun at that spot hour by hour for the whole period, 2021 to 2031. If no part of the Sun is above the horizon in any hour, the spot sparkles and says: "This spot did not see the Sun once from 2021 to 2031 in our model. Water ice could hide in places like this." with one line of NASA context (in 2009 the LCROSS mission hit the shadowed crater Cabeus and uncovered water). If the Sun does reach the spot, it says for how much of the time. A counter keeps the dark spots found in this hunt (at least 5 km apart); pressing "Ice hunter" again stops the hunt and clears the points and the count, so the map is clean for the next one.
17. **Quiz.** The "Quiz" button opens the "Moon quiz": five questions drawn at random from a pool of 25, four answers each, in English, Russian or Kyrgyz. After each answer: "Correct!" or "Not quite", with the reason the right answer is right and the reason the chosen one is wrong, in plain words for students. Fifteen questions are facts about the app and the challenge (what a green block means, why Earth is up only part of the month, the 354 day repeat, what Chandrayaan-3 and IM-2 showed, what LCROSS found). Ten are marked "Real data from the app": each takes a real prepared site and a real day and gets its right answer from the same survival rule as the grid, so it is always true to the app. The round ends with a score out of five and "play again".

**How it works.** An offline Python pipeline traces the horizon at each site on NASA LOLA `LDEM_80S_80M` terrain (80 m per pixel) every 0.25 degrees of direction, out to 300 km, with the Moon's curvature. In the browser, Sun and Earth positions for any moment come from astronomy-engine with the IAU lunar rotation model. The app compares them with the horizon hour by hour for 14 days and applies the rules. The four games for students use that same hourly Sun and Earth data and the same rules, and each has its own test (`tools/test-lander.cjs`, `tools/test-game.cjs`, `tools/test-ice.cjs`, `tools/test-quiz.cjs`) that opens the app in a browser and compares what the student sees with the model.

**How we know it is right.** The Sun and Earth positions the app shows agree with NASA JPL Horizons to 0.0047 degrees at worst (about a hundredth of the Sun's width), and with an independent JPL DE440 series to under 0.01 degrees; the offline terrain pipeline agrees with Horizons to 0.47 arcseconds. At Chandrayaan-3's site our sunset is 4 September 2023, the day ISRO put the lander to sleep; at Chang'e 6's site Earth never rises, and the real mission used a relay satellite. Against NASA Goddard's polar illumination maps at twenty points, our Earth in view share differs by 3.3 points on average (r 0.97). Every verdict is also judged four more ways (a day earlier, a day later, the terrain 0.2° higher, the terrain 0.2° lower) and marked solid or borderline, and the 1 km neighbour check agrees with the app's own "add a point by coordinates" path on 80 of 80 verdicts. Each of the five additions (score and rank, solid or borderline, the 1 km check, the dish line, the motion) has its own test.

**Limits we state.** On sharp ridges our share of time in sunlight is higher than NASA's maps (11 points on average): read ridge numbers as an upper estimate. A lander you drop yourself is judged on coarser terrain (320 m per pixel instead of 80 m), so narrow peaks lose detail and it usually finds fewer safe days than the prepared sites. The model judges a place and a day, not a touchdown: it passes IM-2's site and day, and the real lander fell on its side. The battery and radio limits (2 and 3 days) are our teaching values, not NASA requirements; for scale, NASA says its VIPER rover cannot endure more than 50 hours of continuous darkness. The score is a share of days (green landing days divided by all landing days in the period shown, times 100), not a science score; it moves with the period and with the battery and radio limits. "Solid" means only that the answer held in the four re-judgements (a day earlier or later, the terrain 0.2° higher or lower); the 0.2° is our own test value, not a NASA figure. The 8 spots 1 km around a target are judged on NASA LOLA terrain averaged to 320 m, coarser than the 80 m of the prepared sites, so that check tests a 1 km miss, not 100 m, and works only within about 235 km of the south pole; it needs a 4.5 MB terrain file on its first use, and on a slow connection the panel can say "Checking the 8 spots 1 km around…" for up to half a minute (measured 1, 2 and 24 seconds from Naryn) while the rest of the answer is already there. The dish line is geometry only: the 10° above the horizon is our planning value, not a NASA rule; it is not a booking and does not know whether a dish is free; it never changes the verdict; and it names the dishes that have the Moon up at the landing hour even when Earth is not in view from the site at that hour. Our terrain is 80 m per pixel and some other projects use finer terrain, so a rock or small crater below 80 m is not in our data. The comparison with other projects (no other project in Kyrgyz, no other game where a student builds a lander) holds only for the 512 submissions to our challenge that we read on 15 November, and it comes from each team's own submission text, not from running their apps.

**Limits of the four games, stated plainly.**
- A spot the student picks (a lander dropped by clicking the Moon, or an Ice hunter tap) is judged on NASA LOLA terrain averaged to 320 m, coarser than the 80 m of the fifteen prepared sites, and only within about 235 km of the south pole. Small shadows and small sunlit peaks are missed, so a spot the model calls dark could have a lit peak, and a spot it calls lit could be shaded. Farther from the pole, Ice hunter says "Too far from the pole" and gives no verdict.
- Ice hunter does not detect ice. It says water ice "could" hide where our model never sees the Sun, nothing more; only a spacecraft that goes there can tell. The Sun counts as reaching a spot when any part of its disc is above the horizon in an hour. A spot the Sun does reach is called "too warm for ice to stay": that is a simple rule, not a temperature model.
- Keep me alive uses the team's teaching rule, not a real power system: a full battery lasts the battery limit (2 days) in the dark, one hour of Sun refills it at once, and the lander arrives with 23 hours of charge (with the default limits). The battery limit (2 days), the radio limit (3 days) and the 24 hour first Sun limit are our example values, not NASA requirements; the student can change the first two on the map. The game counts the Sun as up when at least half of its disc is above the horizon, the same test as the verdict.
- In the Quiz, the ten "Real data from the app" questions are asked about the prepared sites and days between 2022 and 2030, and answered by the survival rule, so they are as right as the grid is. The other fifteen are fixed texts written from the app's documents and NASA and mission pages that the code names; they are not computed, so they are only as right as those pages and our reading of them.
- Like the whole app, the games judge a place and a day, not a touchdown: the drop animation and the game show the verdict, they do not simulate landing a spacecraft.
- The lander models, the hats and every drawing in the games are made by the team in code (three.js shapes and canvas patterns, written with AI help). They are simplified, recognisable drawings, not photos, and they show no real person. The Russian and Kyrgyz texts of the games were drafted with AI, and the check by the team's native speakers is still to be done.

**Benefits.** Students in Kyrgyzstan and Central Asia can learn, in their own language, how real Moon missions are planned and why only some days work; a teacher gets a ready lesson that turns Moon geometry into a yes or no answer. When a launch slips, a planner sees in one click which other days and sites still work, whether an answer is solid or borderline, and what a 1 km miss would do.

## NASA Data

- NASA LRO LOLA `LDEM_80S_80M` south polar terrain, PDS Geosciences Node: the terrain, horizons and map.
- NASA M2M-30044 Moon to Mars Lunar Surface Data Book (2025), https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20250002845 : the six candidate site coordinates.
- NASA JPL DE440 ephemeris and lunar orientation kernels (NAIF): Sun, Earth and Moon positions.
- NASA JPL Horizons, https://ssd.jpl.nasa.gov/horizons/ : independent check of every Sun and Earth position.
- NASA Goddard PGDA Lunar Polar Illumination maps, https://pgda.gsfc.nasa.gov/products/69 : check of our sunlight and Earth view shares.
- NASA, Artemis Human Landing System Overview, https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20250009323 : the Artemis III landing site rules we use (within 6 degrees of the pole, landing slope under 10 degrees, slope within 2 km under 20 degrees).
- NASA Scientific Visualization Studio CGI Moon Kit (LROC colour map) and Blue Marble: the pictures of the Moon and Earth.
- LROC team images locating IM-2 and Chandrayaan-3: https://lroc.im-ldi.com/images/1408 and https://lroc.im-ldi.com/images/1314
- NASA DSN Now, https://eyes.nasa.gov/dsn/config.xml : the positions of the three Deep Space Network sites (Goldstone, Madrid, Canberra) for the "Dish on Earth" line.
- NASA, What is the Deep Space Network?, https://www.nasa.gov/directorates/somd/space-communications-navigation-program/what-is-the-deep-space-network/ : the three sites are placed "approximately 120 degrees apart in longitude".
- NASA VIPER lunar operations page, for the 50 hours of darkness figure.
- NASA Science, LCROSS mission page, https://science.nasa.gov/mission/lcross/ : the one line of context in Ice hunter (LCROSS hit the shadowed crater Cabeus in 2009 and uncovered water near the Moon's south pole).
